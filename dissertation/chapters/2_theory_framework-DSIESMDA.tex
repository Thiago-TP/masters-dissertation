\section{DSI-ESMDA}
\subsection{Key concepts and definitions}
This subsection presents the key mathematical objects and concepts 
used by both the standard DSI-ESMDA and the proposed Gated DSI-ESMDA,
many of which are related to the forward model 
presented in \cref{sec:forward-model}.
%
Important nuances, such as the distinction between prediction and forecasting,
are also pointed here.

\subsubsection{Predicting versus forecasting}
Prediction and forecasting, usually interchangeable terms, 
have an important distinction in this work.
%
Prediction refers to the estimation of any datum at a given time,
while forecasting refers to the estimation of any datum beyond the observation period.
%
Forecasting is a type of prediction, but in particular prediction of new data.

In Data Science literature, 
a common way of estimating forecast quality is splitting available data 
into a training partition and a test partition.
%
The training partition is used to freely adjust predictions within a given period, 
and the test, to verify how close predictions will be on new data.

In the context of this work, data from a given reference will be split into two disjointed periods in time:
history period, and validation period,
mirroring the common train and test splits respectively.

\subsubsection{Data vectors}
Let $\mathbf{d}$ denote the true production data vector of a reference reservoir across time,
free of observation error.
As discussed above, data is separated into a history period and a validation period,
so that $\mathbf{d} = \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{d}^\top_h & \mathbf{d}^\top_v
\end{bmatrix}^\top$
with $\mathbf{d}_h$ the ground-truth history data, and $\mathbf{d}_v$ the ground-truth validation data.
%
Analogously, let $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{m}$ be 
the controls applied to the ground-truth and the parameters of the ground-truth, respectively.
%
Denoting the prediction forward model by $\mathbf{g}(\cdot, \cdot)$,
ground-truth data is given by $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{u})$.

Let $\mathbf{d}_j$ denote the production data vector produced by 
running the forward model with parameters $\mathbf{m}_j$
from the start of the history period up to (at least) the end of the validation period,
i.e., $\mathbf{d}_j = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{m}_j, \mathbf{u})$.
For an ensemble of $N_e$ realizations, there are $N_e$ different such vectors, 
each of which associated to its own parameters vector.

Following the pattern established in the ground-truth, 
$\mathbf{d}_{h,\ j}$ denotes the history period data output by realization $j$.
Assume $\mathbf{d}_h$ is corrupted by additive measurement noise $\mathbf{e}_d\sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{C}_e)$,
where $\mathbf{C}_e$ is the noise covariance matrix.
The corrupted vector is the oberved data during the history period, denoted $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$.
%

\subsection{Statistical Framework}
\label{subsec:statistical-framework}
This subsection details the statistical frameworks underpinning the DSI-ESMDA algorithm,
and expects the reader to hold knowledge of probability theory, namely:
the axioms of probability; 
what is a conditional probability; 
statement of Bayes' theorem.
what is a probability distribution; 
formulation of Gaussian probability density function.
%
\Cref{subsec:fopts} also assume knowledge of cumulative distribution function. 
%
\citeonline[appendix C]{Emerick:2025} provides a brief overview of all these topics.

DSI-ESMDA can be conceptulized as a Bayesian approach to the forecasting problem,
aiming to estimate the underlying conditional probability distribution of 
predictions given the reference data.
%
For given data, the algorithm returns samples from this posterior distribution,
outputing a range of values rather than a single value.
%
While this makes evaluating the algorithm less straightforward, 
it allows for quantification of uncertainty.

Let $p(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}_\text{obs})$ 
be the conditional \emph{a posteriori} probability distribution
of true reservoir production being $\mathbf{d}$ given observations $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$.
%
From Bayes' theorem, one can write the posterior as
%
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}_\text{obs}) =
    c_\text{pos} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} \mid \mathbf{d}) p(\mathbf{d})
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}  
%
where $c_\text{pos}$ is, for the purposes of this work, a normalization constant;
$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}\mid \mathbf{d})$,
the likelihood function%
\footnote{%
    Note that the likelihood is denoted by a $\mathcal{L}$ instead of the usual $p$.
    This is deliberate, since likelihoods are not necessarily probability distributions, 
    i.e., they need not sum up to 1. 
}, 
is the probability of $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$ being the true reservoir production 
if $\mathbf{d}$ was observed, and;
$p(\mathbf{d})$, the prior, is the probability of observing $\mathbf{d}$
before observations $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$ were made.

Maximizing \cref{eq:bayes} means finding reservoir production that is 
best suited to match field observations tempered by prior beliefs on the behavior of data.
%    
While this approach is statistically sensible, in practice a single maximization tends to produce poor matches to data.
%
This situation could be mitigated by assimilating more data, 
since then the posterior would be adjusted Bayes' theorem,
$p(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{d}^\text{new}_\text{obs}, \mathbf{d}_\text{obs}) = 
c^\text{new}_\text{pos} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^\text{new}_\text{obs}\mid \mathbf{d}) p(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{d}_\text{obs})$,
but it is assumed that $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$ already holds all the data available for analysis.

Therefore, in order to improve data match, the effect of posterior updating is obtained 
by successively assimilating disturbed versions of observed data.
%
This is called Multiple Data Assimilation (MDA), contrasting with the previous Single Data Assimilation (SDA).
%
Denoting the number of assimilations by $N_a$,
assimilation $1\leq l\leq N_a$ with disturbed observations $\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs}$
produces posterior $p^l$ such that%
\footnote{%
    Writing a conditional distribution as the product of simpler conditional distributions 
    is sometimes called  the chain rule of conditional probabilities
    \cite[subsection C.1.8]{Emerick:2025}.
}
%
\begin{align}
    p^1(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}) 
    &= c^1_\text{pos} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}\mid \mathbf{d}) 
                        p(\mathbf{d}) 
\label{eq:p1}
    \\
    p^2(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}^2_\text{obs}, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}) 
    &= c^2_\text{pos} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^2_\text{obs}\mid \mathbf{d}) 
                        p^1(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}) 
\label{eq:p2} 
    \\
    &\ \ \vdots 
    \\
    p^{N_a}(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}^{N_a}_\text{obs}, \dots, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs})
    &= c^{N_a}_{\text{pos},\mathcal{L}_\text{pos}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^{N_a}_\text{obs} \mid \mathbf{d})
        p^{N_a-1}(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{d}^{N_a-1}_\text{obs}, \dots, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}) 
\label{eq:pNa} 
    \\
    &= p(\mathbf{d}) \prod_{l=1}^{N_a} c^l_\text{pos} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs} \mid \mathbf{d}) 
\label{eq:p-prod}
\end{align}  

By successively updating the posterior with disturbed data, the posterior's maximum is also successively updated.
If maximizing \cref{eq:bayes} was akin to trying to reach optimum $p^*$ in a single fixed step,
maximizing \cref{eq:p-prod} is akin to reaching $p^*$ with multiple steps, 
as illustrated in \cref{fig:sda-versus-mda}.
The number of steps is exactly equal to $N_a$, and their size dependent on the perturbations done to the observed data. 
%
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering 
    \caption{%
        Posterior distributions obtained with SDA (green) and MDA (yellow) 
        for an observation vector with 2 points of data.
        Circles denotes means, and circunferences, covariances.
        True underlying posterior is denoted in blue, and the prior in gray. 
    }
    \label{fig:sda-versus-mda}
    \input{figures/sda-versus-mda.tikz}
\end{figure}

While SDA and MDA can be made to be equivalent if likelihoods are Gaussian 
(more details in \cref{subsec:update-equation}), 
in general MDA produces better matches to data than SDA.
This is because MDA attempts to reduce variance in the posterior distribution 
without underestimating uncertainty in observations, 
which often results in tighter posteriors whose means are closer aligned to observations.
%
Furthermore, the iterative search of MDA for a maximum 
better acommodates for underlying likelihoods that are not Gaussian.

\subsection{Deriving the update equation}
\label{subsec:update-equation}
This subsection derives the base update equation used by DSI-ESMDA 
(and by extension, Gated DSI-ESMDA) 
by extracting a practical implementation from the general, more abstract 
statistical framework outlined in \cref{subsec:statistical-framework}.
%
Chiefly, this process requires making assumptions on prior and likelihoods 
and then using model ensembles to sample from the distributions. 

In the context of reservoir engineering, it is standard to assume that data 
(and model parameters, for that matter)
have underlying Gaussian distributions.
%
In this case, the general likelihood in \cref{eq:bayes} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} \mid \mathbf{d}) =
    c_\mathcal{L} \exp(-\mathcal{O}_d(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}))
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{O}_d$ is defined as
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}_d(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}) =
    \frac{1}{2} 
    (\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})^\top 
    \mathbf{C}_e
    (\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}),
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathbf{C}_e$ the measurement noise in observed data.
%
The associated posterior can then be rewritten as 
%
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{d}_\text{obs}) = 
    c_\text{pos} 
    c_\mathcal{L} 
    p(\mathbf{d}) 
    \exp(-\mathcal{O}_d(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})).
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}


Similarly, the likelihood at assimilation $l$ in \cref{eq:p-prod} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^l(\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}^l \mid \mathbf{d}) =
    c^l_\mathcal{L} \exp(-\mathcal{O}^l_d(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}))
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{O}^l_d$ is defined as
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}^l_d(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}) =
    \frac{1}{2} 
    (\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})^\top 
    \mathbf{C}^l_e
    (\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}),
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathbf{C}^l_e$ the measurement noise in the disturbed observed data.
%
The associated posterior can then be rewritten as 
%
\begin{align}
    p^{N_a}(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{d}^{N_a}_\text{obs}, \dots, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}) 
    &=
    p(\mathbf{d}) 
    \prod_{l=1}^{N_a} 
        c^l_\text{pos} 
        \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs}\mid \mathbf{d}) \\
    &=
    p(\mathbf{d}) 
    \prod_{l=1}^{N_a} 
        c^l_\text{pos} 
        c^l_\mathcal{L}
        \exp(-\mathcal{O}^l_d(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})) \\
    &=
    c^{N_a}_{\text{pos},\mathcal{L}} 
    p(\mathbf{d})
    \exp\left(
        -\sum_{l=1}^{N_a} \mathcal{O}^l_d(\mathbf{d}^l_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})
    \right) 
\label{eq:posterior-MDA}
\end{align}
%
where $c^{N_a}_{\text{pos},\mathcal{L}}$ is a normalization constant that 
absorbed the intermediary posterior and likelihood normalization constants.
%
Then, \cref{eq:posterior-MDA} is equivalent to \cref{eq:posterior} if and only if 
%
\begin{equation}
    c^{N_a}_{\text{pos},\mathcal{L}} = c_\text{pos} c_\mathcal{L}, 
    \quad 
    \sum_{l=1}^{N_a} (\mathbf{C}^l_e)^{-1} = \mathbf{C}^{-1}_e.
\label{eq:cov-requirement}
\end{equation}

Now, DSI-ESMDA models the covariance at assimilation $l$ proportionately to $\mathbf{C}_e$:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{C}^l_e = \alpha_l \mathbf{C}_e, 
    \quad 
    \alpha_l > 0,
\label{eq:cov-as-inflation}
\end{equation}
%
so that \cref{eq:cov-requirement} reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{l=1}^{N_a} \frac{1}{\alpha_l} = 1.
\label{eq:alpha-requirement}
\end{equation}

Constants $\alpha_l$ parameterize the covariance in assimilation $l$,
and by extension how much the intermediary posterior distribution should tighten around the data.
%
Invoking the analogy at the end of \cref{subsec:statistical-framework} 
and the associated illustration in \cref{fig:sda-versus-mda}, 
$\mathbf{C}_e$ influences the direction of the adjustment,
facilitating corrections on less uncertain data,
and $\alpha_l$ influences the size of the adjustment, 
so that assimilations with larger $\alpha_l$ incur smaller corrections.  

The next step towards an implementable update equation is assuming the prior distribution is also Gaussian, i.e.,
%
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{d}) = c_\text{prior} \exp(-\mathcal{O}_p(\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}_\text{prior}))
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
%
with $c_\text{prior}$ a normalization constant and
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}_p(\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}_\text{prior}) = 
    \frac{1}{2}
    (\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}_\text{prior})^\top 
    \mathbf{C}_p^{-1}
    (\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}_\text{prior})
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{d}_\text{prior}$ is the prediction with highest belief of occurring prior to observing $\mathbf{d}_\text{obs}$,
and $\mathbf{C}_p$ is the prior covariance matrix, i.e., it is assumed that 
$\mathbf{d} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{d}_\text{prior}, \mathbf{C}_p)$.

Substituting \cref{eq:prior} in \cref{eq:posterior-MDA}, 
it follows that the first assimilation (\cref{eq:p1}) can be written as
%
\begin{align}
    p^1(\mathbf{d} \mid \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs})
    &=
    c^1 \exp\left(
        -[
            \mathcal{O}_p(\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior}) + 
            \mathcal{O}^1_d(\mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d})
        ]
    \right) \\
    &=
    c^1 \exp(-\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{d}, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}, \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior}))
\label{eq:posterior-MDA-1}
\end{align} 
%
where $c^1 = c_\text{prior} c^1_\text{pos} c^1_{\mathcal{L}}$,
and $\mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior}$ is the mean of the prior distribution after 0 assimilations,
i.e., before any assimilation was performed.
%
The maximum \emph{a posterior} (MAP) of the first assimilation,
denoted $\mathbf{d}^1_\text{map}$, is thus given by 
%
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{d}^1_\text{map} 
    &= \argmin_\mathbf{d} (
        \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{d}, \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}, \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior})
    )
    \\
    0 &= \nabla_\mathbf{d}\mathcal{O}(
        \mathbf{d}^1_\text{map}, 
        \mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs}, 
        \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior}
    ) 
    \\
    0 &=
    \mathbf{C}_p^{-1} (\mathbf{d}^1_\text{map} - \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior})
    -
    \mathbf{C}_e^{-1} (\mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}^1_\text{map})
    \\
    0 &= 
    \mathbf{C}_p^{-1} (\mathbf{d}^1_\text{map} - \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior})
    -
    \mathbf{C}_e^{-1} (\mathbf{d}^1_\text{obs} - \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior} - 
        [\mathbf{d}^1_\text{map} - \mathbf{d}^0_\text{prior}]
    )
\end{align}

DSI-ESMDA updates the ensemble outputs by iterating a Kalman-like data assimilation, shown below
\cite{Lima:2020}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{d}_j^{k+1} 
    =
    \mathbf{d}_j^k + (\mathbf{R} \circ \mathbf{K}^k) 
    (\mathbf{d}_\text{obs} + \sqrt{\alpha_k}\mathbf{e}_j^k - \mathbf{d}_{h,\ j}^k),
\label{eq:iteration_dsiesmda}
\end{equation}
%
with $1 \leq k \leq N_a$, $N_a$ the number of assimilations DSI-ESMDA must do.
Thus, $\mathbf{d}_j^k$ for $k=1$ is the prior ensemble (initially denoted $\mathbf{d}_j$),
and $\mathbf{d}_j^{Na+1}$, the posterior.
%
Vector $\mathbf{e}_j^k\sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{C}_e)$ is a random disturbance,
and $\alpha_k$ coefficients are positive constants that satisfy $\sum_k 1/\alpha_k = 1$. 

Matrices $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are the localization and the modified Kalman gain, respectively.
$\mathbf{K}$ is defined as
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{K}^k = 
    \Delta\mathbf{D}^k (\Delta\mathbf{D}_h^k)^\top
    \left(
        \Delta\mathbf{D}_h^k (\Delta\mathbf{D}_h^k)^\top
        +
        \alpha_k\mathbf{C}_e
    \right)^{-1},
\end{equation}
%
with 
%
\begin{align}
    \Delta\mathbf{D} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_e-1}} 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{d}_1 - \overline{\mathbf{d}} &
        \cdots &
        \mathbf{d}_{N_e} - \overline{\mathbf{d}}
    \end{bmatrix}, 
\label{eq:delta-d}
    \\
    \overline{\mathbf{d}} &= \frac{1}{N_e} 
    \sum_{j=1}^{N_e} \mathbf{d}_j,
\end{align}
a matrix that quantifies the spread of the ensemble. 
$\Delta \mathbf{D}_h$ is defined analogously by constraining the above equations to the history matching period:
%
\begin{align}
    \Delta\mathbf{D}_h &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_e-1}} 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{d}_{h, 1} - \overline{\mathbf{d}}_h &
        \cdots &
        \mathbf{d}_{h, N_e} - \overline{\mathbf{d}}_h
    \end{bmatrix}, 
    \\
    \overline{\mathbf{d}}_h &= \frac{1}{N_e} 
    \sum_{j=1}^{N_e} \mathbf{d}_{h, j}.
\end{align}

\begin{note}[Index $v$]
    Data vectors stack time series of a fields' measurements, 
    so index $v$ in this chapter's equations specify a datum in place and time simultaneously.
    %
    For example, if DSI is being applied on the oil production of two wells of a field, 
    each with a simulation time vector of 100 elements, 
    then for any $1\leq j\leq N_e$ $\mathbf{d}_j$ would be 200$\times$1, 
    with elements $v=1$ through $v=100$ the oil production history of the first well,
    and elements $v=101$ through $v=200$ the oil production history of the second well.
\end{note}

\begin{note}[Time vectors]
    Note that all realization data vectors are implicitly assumed to have a synchronized time vector.
    Ultimately, this means simulation scheduling was the same for all realizations. 
    %
    Reference data, however, is under no obligation to follow this scheduling.
    %
    As such, in order for \cref{eq:iteration_dsiesmda} to work,
    the history period of the realization data is constrained to match the observed data's.
    %
    This leads to an important practical requirement of the algorithm:
    for simulations to cover the time vector of observed data.
    %
    \Cref{fig:time-vector-illustration} illustrates this process 
    for data vectors with a single feature (e.g., field oil production total). 
    %
    \begin{center}

    \end{center}
\end{note}

\subsection{Improving data match with covariance localization}
$\mathbf{R}$ adjusts the Kalman gain, 
preserving correlated measures while 
diminishing the impact of spuriously correlated measures. 
As such, its entries lie in $[0, 1]$, and pointwisely multiply $\mathbf{K}$.
Intuitively, a correlation is strong if measures of a same quantity were taken closer in space and/or in time, and weaker otherwise. 
Denoting this ``distance'' by $\delta_{uv}$, 
where $u$ is an index from $\mathbf{d}_j$ and 
$v$ an index from $\mathbf{d}_h$, 
$\mathbf{R}$ is given by
%
\begin{align}
    R_{uv} &= r(\delta_{uv}), 
    \\
    r(\delta) &= \begin{cases}
        -\frac{1}{4}\delta^5 + \frac{1}{2}\delta^4 + \frac{5}{8}\delta^3 -\frac{5}{3}\delta^2 + 1,
        & 0 \leq \delta \leq 1 \\
        \frac{1}{12}\delta^5 - \frac{1}{2}\delta^4 + \frac{5}{8}\delta^3 +\frac{5}{3}\delta^2 -5\delta + 4 - \frac{2}{3}\delta^{-1},
        & 1 < \delta \leq 2 \\
        0,
        & \delta > 2 
    \end{cases},
    \\
    \delta_{uv} &= 
    \sqrt{
        \left(\frac{\Delta x'_{uv}}{L_{x'}}\right)^2 + 
        \left(\frac{\Delta y'_{uv}}{L_{y'}}\right)^2 + 
        \left(\frac{\Delta t_{uv}}{T_c}\right)^2
    }, \\
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \Delta x'_{uv} \\ \Delta y'_{uv}
    \end{bmatrix}
    &= 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 
        \sin\theta & \cos\theta
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \Delta x_{uv} \\ \Delta y_{uv}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

$\Delta x_{uv}$ and $\Delta y_{uv}$ are the $x$ and $y$ differences, respectively, 
between the points in space from where measure $u$ and $v$ were taken.
In this context, measurements always come from a well, and so these differences are always distances between wells.
%
$\Delta t_{uv}$ is the difference between the time when measurement $u$ was taken, and the time when $v$ was taken.
Note that while $v$ is restrained to the history matching period by definition, $u$ is not.
The inclusion of $\Delta t_{uv}$ guarantees that measurements far apart in time will hold little correlation,
even if taken from the same well.

A rotation by $\theta$ is applied to take into account the anisotropy of reservoir permeability,
and are $L_{x'}$, $L_{y'}$, $T_c$ positive constants that regulate the critical points of differences in $x$, $y$ and $t$ separately.
%
By critical, it is understood the inflection point $\delta=1$ in $r(\delta)$.
%
As illustrated by \cref{fig:compact-correlation-function}, the greater the critical value, 
the more lax the localization, allowing more weakly correlated changes to hold more impact.
