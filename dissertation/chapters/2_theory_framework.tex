\debug{%
The theory framework is the scaffolding upon which your thesis is built. 
When you're done writing your theory framework chapter or section, your reader should be able to answer these questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What theoretical concepts are used in the research? What hypotheses, if any, are you using?
    \item Why have you chosen this theory?
    \item What are the implications of using this theory?
    \item How does the theory relate to the existing literature, your problem statement and your epistemological and ontological positions?
    \item How has this theory has been applied by others in similar contexts? What can you learn from them and how do you differ?
    \item How do you apply the theory and measure the concepts (with reference to the literature review/problem statement)?
    \item What is the relationship between the various elements and concepts within the model? Can you depict this visually?
\end{itemize}
}

\debug{%
That means that a theory framework can take different forms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It can state the theoretical assumptions underpinning the study.
    \item It can connect the empirical data to existing knowledge.
    \item It can allow you to come up with propositions, concepts or hypotheses that you can use to answer ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions.
\end{itemize}
}

\section{Prediction forward model}
\label{sec:forward-model}
The aims of this section are twofold:
to introduce the concept of prediction forward model and other related concepts;
to introducing notation that will be assumed known after this section.

As discussed in \cref{subsec:inverse-problem}, 
the inverse problem is part of the process in obtaining forecasts.
%
Denoting by $\mathbf{m}$ the vector of model parameters,
and by $\mathbf{u}$ the set of controls applied on the field,
and by $\mathbf{d}$ the reservoir production within the time frame of the controls,
one can write
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{u}; \mathbf{x}_0)
\label{eq:prediction-forward-model}
\end{equation} 
%
with $\mathbf{x}_0$ the initial value of reservoir states.
Since $\mathbf{x}_0$ is constant, it is omitted in the remainder of this work for simplification.

Function $\mathbf{g}$ is called the \emph{prediction forward model}, or simply forward model
\cite[section 1.4]{Emerick:2025}.
%
The forward model is the underlying mapping between a 
model's parameters, controls and initial states to its observable features.
%
In this work, the features employed are 
oil and water production rates (OPR and WPR);
water injection rates (WIR);
bottomhole pressures (BHP), i.e., the pressure at a well's completion point;
water cut percentages (WCTP), i.e., the volume of water relative to the volume of fluid extracted by a producer well, and;
field oil production total (FOPT), i.e., the total volume of oil extracted from the field within the given time period.

The key assumption behind the forward model is that it is deterministic. 
Implementation wise, $\mathbf{g}$ consists of evaluating a set of equations 
at each gridblock of the model.
%
Each evaluation evolves the model a little further in time, 
until the scheduled time period is appreciated, concluding the process.
%
For simplicity, the described process will be referred to as a simulation of the reservoir.

In truth, no reservoir simulator can be an unerrant representation of underlying reservoir dynamics, 
unless the reservoir itself is extremely simple.
%
Still, the mathematical equations that constitute the forward model are give reasonable approximations to these dynamics,
so that data mismatches are mostly caused by model parameters unrepresentative of their true values.

With that said, simulators vary in which equations are used.
Of the options available, the most commonly used are black-oil equations
\cite{Rasmussen:2021}.
Based on the premise that there are three different fluid phases (aqueous, oleic, and gaseous) 
and three (pseudo)components (water, oil, and gas),
the equations can be deduced from conservation of mass for each component with suitable closure relationships. 
%
A given phase $\alpha$'s component mass $A_\alpha$ satisfies the conservation equation
%
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left(\phi_{\text{ref}} A_\alpha \right)
    + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}_\alpha + q_\alpha & = 0,
\label{eq:materialbalance-cont}
\end{align}
%
where 
%
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:acc}
\begin{align}
  A_w &= m_{\phi} b_{w} s_{w},                          & \mathbf{u}_w &= b_{w} \mathbf{v}_{w}, \\
  A_o &= m_{\phi} (b_{o} s_{o} + r_{og}b_{g}s_{g}), & \mathbf{u}_o &= b_{o} \mathbf{v}_{o} +
r_{og}b_{g}\mathbf{v}_{g}, \\
  A_g &= m_{\phi} (b_{g} s_{g} + r_{go}b_{o}s_{o}), & \mathbf{u}_g &= b_{g} \mathbf{v}_{g} +
r_{go}b_{o}\mathbf{v}_{o}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
and the phase fluxes are given by Darcy's law:
%
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{v}_\alpha = -\lambda_\alpha \mathbf{K} (\nabla p_\alpha - \rho_\alpha \mathbf{g}).
    \label{eq:Darcy-mph}
\end{align}
%
Finally, the following closure equations are imposed:
%
\begin{align}
    s_w + s_o + s_g &= 1, \label{eq:fluids-fill-porevolume} \\
    p_{c,ow} & = p_{o} - p_{w}, \label{eq:oil-water-capillary} \\
    p_{c,og} & = p_{o} - p_{g}. \label{eq:oil-gas-capillary}
\end{align} 

The description of each symbol is given below 
\cite[adapted]{Rasmussen:2021}.

\begin{description}
    \item[$m_\phi$] pore volume multiplier as function of pressure.
    \item[$\phi_{\text{ref}}$] reference porosity, a constant in time
    but varying in space.
    \item[$p_\alpha$] phase pressure for phase $\alpha$.
    \item[$b_\alpha$] shrinkage/expansion factor for phase $\alpha$ defined as the ratio of surface volume at standard conditions to reservoir volume for a given amount of fluid:
        $b_\alpha = V_{\text{surface}, \alpha}/V_{\text{reservoir}, \alpha}$.
        For the oil phase, $b_o$ is called shrinkage factor, 
        whereas $b_g$ is called expansion factor.
    \item[$r_{go}$] ratio of dissolved gas to oil in the oleic phase.
    \item[$r_{og}$] ratio of vaporized oil to gas in the gaseous phase.
    \item[$s_\alpha$] saturation of phase $\alpha$, the pore volume fraction occupied by the phase. The saturations of all phases sum to 1.
    \item[$p_{c,\alpha \beta}$] capillary pressure between phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
    \item[$\mathbf{K}$] permeability of the porous medium.
    \item[$k_{r,\alpha}$] relative permeability for phase $\alpha$, modeling the reduction in effective permeability for a fluid phase in the presence of other phases.
    \item[$\mu_\alpha$] viscosity of phase $\alpha$, typically a function of phase pressure and composition.
    \item[$\lambda_\alpha$] mobility of phase $\alpha$, given by $\lambda_\alpha = k_{r,\alpha}/\mu_\alpha$.
    \item[$\mathbf{v}_\alpha$] phase velocity of phase $\alpha$.
    \item[$\mathbf{u}_\alpha$] component velocity of component $\alpha$.
    \item[$\rho_{S, \alpha}$] surface density of phase $\alpha$ at one atmosphere, a given constant.
    \item[$\rho_\alpha$] density of phase $\alpha$ in the reservoir. 
        For water, $\rho_w = b_w\rho_{S,w}$. 
        For the oleic phase the relationship is more complex since it must include dissolved gas: 
            $\rho_o = b_o(\rho_{S,o} + r_{go}\rho_{S,g})$, 
        similar for the gaseous phase: 
            $\rho_g = b_g(\rho_{S,g} + r_{og}\rho_{S,o})$.
    \item[$\mathbf{g}$] gravitational acceleration vector.
\end{description}
