\section{Reservoir case studies}
    Three reservoirs were used as case studies for this work, two of which are synthetic reservoirs and one is a real reservoir. 
    This section presents them, highlighting their most significant structural and geological characteristics within the scope of this work. 
    However, as information regarding the real reservoir is sensitive, it is only briefly discussed.

    The choice of these reservoirs was based on the availability of data and their suitability for testing the proposed methods:
    the Egg and Olympus ensembles are widely used in the literature for benchmarking purposes (with the Olympus ensemble being the more complex of the two),
    while the real field provides a practical application scenario for the algorithms employed in this work.

    Simulations of the Egg and Olympus ensembles to generate the associated priors 
    were performed using the JutulDarcy.jl simulator \cite{Moyner:2025}.

    \subsection{Egg ensemble}
    Originally introduced as a deterministic model in a study aimed at 
    exploring the reasons behind reservoir flooding problems and identifying conditions for optimal solutions 
    \cite{Zandvliet:2007}, 
    a stochastic version was later proposed as a benchmark in a robust waterflooding optimization problem \cite{Essen:2009}. 
    This reservoir consists of 18,533 active cells with dimensions of 8x8x4 m. 
    The arrangement of these cells forms an egg-shaped grid, hence the name \cite{Jansen:2014}.

        \subsubsection{Realizations}

        In addition to eight injectors labeled INJECT1 through INJECT8, 
        each realization includes four vertical producer wells labeled PROD1 through PROD4, 
        which produce oil and water but not  gas.

    \subsection{Olympus ensemble}
    Launched in early 2017, the Olympus reservoir model was designed as a benchmark environment to problems concerning control optimization and 
    reservoir management within the framework of the Integrated Systems Approach for Petroleum Production 2 research consortium
    \cite{Fonseca:2018,Fonseca:2020}. 
    In what follows, the depiction of Olympus mirrors the one outlined by \citeonline{Fonseca:2020}, with adjustments made where deemed necessary.
        
    Inspired by an oil field in the North Sea, this synthetic reservoir is approximately rectangular, measuring 9x3 km, with a depth of 50 m divided into 16 layers. The 3D grid comprises cells with dimensions of approximately 50x50x3 m, totaling 341,728 cells, of which 192,750 are active. 
    The perimeter of the reservoir is defined by a fault, with six additional smaller faults present within it. 
    Furthermore, Olympus features two distinct zones separated by an impermeable shale layer: 
    The upper zone contains fluvial channel sands embedded in floodplain shales, while the lower zone consists of alternating layers of coarse, medium, and fine sands that are inclined relative to the general structural dip of the field.
    
    The reservoir includes four facies types in different layers (see \cref{tab:olympus-facies-properties}), and geological properties, such as porosity, permeability, and
    the net-to-gross ratio, were generated using standard geostatistical techniques for the different facies types. 
    In the absence of sufficient early-stage data, no porosity/permeability ratio was used. 
    Permeabilities in the $x$ and $y$ directions were identical, while in the $z$ direction, it corresponded to 10\% of that in the $x$ direction.

        \subsubsection{Realizations}
        A set of 50 realizations was generated by varying facies through random seed changes,
        while keeping consistent mesh geometry, faults, and oil-water contact. 
        %
        Uncertain properties such as facies, porosity, permeability, NG ratio,
        initial water saturation distribution, and transmissibility within faults were considered. 

        By first generating a high-fidelity base model with approximately 5 million grid cells,
        5 wells were drilled into this base case model and synthetic records were generated for each of these wells.
        %
        These records were then used to constrain the generation of 50 high-fidelity realizations to capture uncertainty. 
        %
        Each was upscaled for flow simulations using the flow-based upscaling method, 
        featuring a total of 18 wells in each realization, of which 11 are producers and 7 are injectors. 
        %
        Each realization includes 7 injector wells, labeled INJ-$1$ to INJ-$7$, and 11 producer wells, labeled PROD-$1$ to PROD-$11$.
        Producers produce oil and water, but not gas.
        %

        depicted with colors on a logarithmic scale and a fivefold exaggeration of the $z$-axis.

    \subsection{Real field}
    Given that most parameters characterizing the properties of the carbonate reservoir in question 
    cannot be disclosed due to ongoing confidentiality agreements with industrial partners, 
    only essential information is provided.

    The real field is a mature reservoir located in the Brazilian Post-Salt,
    with a production history spanning over 20 years.
    It is comprised of 8 injector wells, labeled I$1$ to I$8$, and 16 producer wells, labeled P$1$ to P$16$.
    Producers produce oil and water, but not gas.

    An ensemble of 200 realizations was generated from observed production data, 
    with a total of 510 simulation time steps of roughly 30 days each.
    Importantly, observed data has a time vector that is not identical to the simulation's but is a subset of it.

    \subsection{Field Oil Production Totals}
    The field oil production total (FOPT) 
    is a measurement commonly used to compare ensembles since ground-truth and realizations are each represented by a single value:
    how much oil was produced within the recorded data period. 
    %
    \Cref{fig:opt-cdf-all} shows the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of field oil production total (FOPT) of the case studies ensembles.
    FOPT values are normalized by the maximum across all a case study's realizations.
    
    The Egg ensemble has a relatively narrow, roughly uniform range of FOPT values,
    suggesting that the priors should be reasonable representatives of the ground-truths, 
    especially those that are closer to the median of the distribution. 
    
    On the other hand,
    the Olympus ensemble has a much wider (about 10 times that of the Egg ensemble), 
    albeit still roughly uniform, range of FOPT values.
    %
    The picked ground-truths (realizations 5, 10, etc.) are spread within the 5-95th percentiles, 
    with half of them quite distant from the median.
    Since this reservoir's FOPTs thin out at the percentile ranges of 0-5\% and 90-100\%,
    it would be expected that ground-truths picked from these ranges would be more difficult to match, 
    as they are less likely to be represented in the prior ensemble.
    This effect is further aggravated by the large range of FOPT values in the Olympus ensemble, which is roughly 10 times larger than that of the Egg ensemble.

    The real ensemble has a similarly wide range of FOPT values.
    However, the distribution quite lopsided, with a very populated 5-80th percentile range and a thin 0-5\% and 80-100\% range.
    As with the Olympus case, a broad range of FOPT values coupled with poorly-represented tails can lead to inadequate priors depending on where the ground-truth sits.

\section{Evaluation workflow and parameters}
\label{sec:evaluation-workflow-parameters}
    While the outline of both DSI-ESMDA and the proposed Gated DSI-ESMDA methods have been presented in \cref{chap:theory-framework},
    it remains to be explained 
    how experiments will be set up (\cref{subsec:evaluation-workflow}), 
    how the error covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}_e$ is constructed (\cref{subsec:noise-modeling}),
    what metrics will be used to evaluate the performance of the methods (\cref{subsec:evaluation-metrics}), and
    what parameters are used (\cref{subsec:evaluation-parameters}). 

    \subsection{Evaluation workflow}
    \label{subsec:evaluation-workflow}
    %
    The evaluation pipeline, illustrated in \cref{fig:flowchart-evaluation}
    is executed once per ground-truth data vector $\mathbf{d}$.
    %
    In synthetic ensembles, 10 ground-truths are picked.
    %
    The average of these results, as well as the results found with the real field case study,
    are presented in the following chapter.
    %
    More detailed results that would not have fit with the flow of the text 
    are exhibited in \cref{chap:detailed-results}. 

    \begin{figure}[htbp]
        \centering
        \caption{%
            Outline of the evaluation process. 
        }
        \label{fig:flowchart-evaluation}
        \input{figures/flowchart-evaluation.tikz}
        \fonte{Self-elaboration.}
    \end{figure}

    First, all data is normalized to the range $[0, 1]$ using the minimum and maximum values of the ground-truth production data, as follows:
    %
    \begin{align}
        \widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{j, f} &= \frac{\mathbf{d}_{j, f} - \min(\mathbf{d}_f)}{\max(\mathbf{d}_f) - \min(\mathbf{d}_f)}
    \label{eq:normalize_realization} \\
        \widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_f      &= \frac{\mathbf{d}_f -      \min(\mathbf{d}_f)}{\max(\mathbf{d}_f) - \min(\mathbf{d}_f)},
    \label{eq:normalize_ground-truth}
    \end{align}
    %
    where index $j$ denotes the realization, $f$ denotes the production data feature, and the tilde indicates normalization.
    This process is repeated for all non-constant features
    and realizations to improve the conditioning of the data and facilitate noise modeling, as shown next.

    \subsection{Noise modeling}
    \label{subsec:noise-modeling}
    %
    The error covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}_e$ is assumed to be diagonal, 
    with its non-zero entries uniformly sampled from $(0, 1]$ and scaled by a constant $\nu > 0$.
    This constant is named the noise magnitude and is a hyperparameter of the Evaluation.
    However, $\nu$ is only means to easier explore the effects of different noise magnitudes on the performance of the methods,
    and not an essential parameter of the methods themselves.

    Next, observed data is constructed by adding noise to the normalized ground-truth data restricted to the history period:
    %
    \begin{equation}
        \widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_\text{obs} = \widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_h + \mathbf{e}, \quad
        \mathbf{e} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{C}_e).
    \end{equation}
    %
    Since real field data already contains noise, this step is skipped for that case, 
    and observed data is simply the normalized ground-truth data restricted to the history period.

    Finally, the methods are applied to the pre-processed data, 
    and their priors and posteriors are evaluated using the metrics described in \cref{subsec:evaluation-metrics}.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{subsec:evaluation-metrics}
    Both the prior and posterior ensembles are evaluated on the history and forecast periods using the following metrics
    \cite[sections 9.4.2 and 9.4.4]{Emerick:2025}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item observation coverage (OC),
        \item mean squared error (MSE),
        \item data-mismatch objective function (denoted $\mathcal{O}$).
    \end{itemize}
    %
    OC measures the percentage of observed data that falls within the range of an ensemble,
    while MSE quantifies the average squared difference between the observed and predicted data.
    DMOF is a metric that quantifies the discrepancy between observed and predicted data.
    Mathematically, each metric is defined as follows:
    %
    \begin{align}
        \text{OC}_h(\mathbf{Y}) &= \frac{1}{N_h} \sum_{i=1}^{N_h} 
        \mathbb{1}\left(
            \min(\mathbf{Y})_i \leq d_{h, i} \leq \max(\mathbf{Y})_i
        \right),\\
        \text{OC}_v(\mathbf{Y}) &= \frac{1}{N_v} \sum_{i=1}^{N_v} 
        \mathbb{1}\left(
            \min(\mathbf{Y})_i \leq d_{v, i} \leq \max(\mathbf{Y})_i
        \right),\\
        %
        \text{MSE}(\mathbf{Y}) &= 
        \frac{1}{N_h} 
        (\mathbf{d}_h - \overline{\mathbf{y}})^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} 
        (\mathbf{d}_h - \overline{\mathbf{y}}),
        \quad 
        \mathbf{C} = \Delta\mathbf{Y}\Delta\mathbf{Y}^\top + \mathbf{C}_e \\
        %
        \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{y}_j) &= 
        \frac{1}{2N_h}
        (\mathbf{d}_h - \mathbf{y}_{j, h})^\top
        \mathbf{C}_e^{-1}
        (\mathbf{d}_h - \mathbf{y}_{j, h}),
    \end{align}
    %
    where 
    %
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 
        $\mathbf{Y} = [\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_{N_e}]$ denotes an ensemble (posterior or prior),
    
        \item
        $N_h$ is the number of data points in the history period, 
    
        \item
        $N_v$ is the number of data points in the validation period, 
    
        \item 
        $\overline{\mathbf{y}}$ is the mean of the ensemble, 
    
        \item 
        $\max(\mathbf{Y})_i$ is the maximum value in the ensemble at datum $i$,
    
        \item 
        $\min(\mathbf{Y})_i$ is the minimum value in the ensemble at datum $i$, 
    
        \item 
        $\Delta\mathbf{Y}$ is the deviation of the predicted data from its mean (see \cref{eq:delta-d}),

        \item $\mathbb{1}: \{0, 1\} \to \{0, 1\}$ returns $1$ if the condition in its argument is true and $0$ otherwise.
    \end{itemize} 

    Both history and validation splits of ground-truth $\mathbf{d}$ are used to compute observation coverages, 
    while MSE and misfit exist only for the history period.
    Despite the similarity between the MSE and the misfit, 
    the latter is computed for each realization in the ensemble,
    while the former is computed for the entire ensemble.
    In this work, the misfit will be presented as an average over all realizations in the ensemble, 
    with an associated standard deviation.

\subsection{Evaluation parameters}
\label{subsec:evaluation-parameters}
    While a straightforward application of DSI-ESMDA requires knowledge of the error covariance matrix 
    (addressed in \cref{subsec:noise-modeling}) and the number of Kalman-like iterations $N_a$,
    the amount of parameters to tune balloon if localization is utilized.
    %
    The proposed Gated DSI-ESMDA method introduces an additional hyperparameter, the gating intensity $\lambda$.
    In practice, the gating is introduced after the standard DSI-ESMDA has been applied and tuned, 
    making the tuning of $\lambda$ incremental and independent of the other parameters.

    \Cref{tab:simulation-parameters-time} compares the time-related simulation parameters and features across reservoirs, 
    while \cref{tab:simulation-parameters-localization} compares the localization-related parameters.
    %
    Note that $N_e$ represents the number of realizations after a ground-truth is separated from the ensemble.
    %
    Experiments were performed
    with the number of iterations fixed at $N_a = 1$ and noise magnitude $\nu=0.01$ for all experiments.
    %
    Both standard DSI-ESMDA and Gated DSI-ESMDA were run with and without localization.
    Finally, the value of gating intensity $\lambda$ varied in $\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$.
